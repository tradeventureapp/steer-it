Party games where your phone is the controller

Nobody keeps eight gamepads in a drawer, but everybody in the room already has a phone. That's the whole idea behind phone-controller party games: one shared screen, and each person's phone becomes their controller — no gamepads, no installs, no setup. Here's how it works and what you need.

How phone-as-controller actually works

One device is the host — the shared screen everyone looks at. It opens the game in a web browser and shows a QR code. Each player scans that code (or opens a short link) with their phone, and their phone turns into a controller: buttons, a touch surface, or tilt, depending on the game. From then on, every phone streams its input to the host in real time, and the action plays out on the big screen for the whole room to watch.

Because it all runs in the browser, there is nothing to download — not on the screen and not on anyone's phone. iPhones and Android phones join the same session side by side, and there's no app to update or account to create just to join in.

Why it beats hunting for gamepads

Physical controllers are the friction that kills a spontaneous games night. You need enough of them, they need charging or fresh batteries, they need pairing, and someone always ends up spectating because "we only have two." Phones remove every part of that. Everyone brings their own controller without knowing it, there's nothing to pair, and the number of players is limited by the game, not by how many pads you own.

How many people can play

It depends on the game, but browser party games commonly support anywhere from a couple of people to eight or more sharing one screen. The sweet spot for most living rooms is somewhere between two and eight — enough for a proper group without anyone losing track of their character on the screen.

What you need to play

  • One screen with a web browser to act as the host — the shared display everyone watches.
  • A phone for each player, with a camera to scan the QR code.
  • An internet connection. Being on the same Wi‑Fi gives the lowest lag, which is the usual setup for a group in one room.

No console, no controllers, no downloads. If you can open a web page, you can play.

Which devices work as the shared screen

Anything with a modern web browser can be the host: a laptop or desktop, a tablet, or a smart TV with a capable browser — and if a TV's own browser can't cope, anything plugged into it (a laptop or a mini-PC) works just as well. The bigger the screen, the better it works for a group — a big screen in the living room turns the whole room into the audience while everyone drives, shoots or draws from their seat.

Which occasions they suit

Phone-controller games shine anywhere a group gathers around one screen and you don't want a setup ritual. House parties and pre-drinks, where people drift in and out and you need something anyone can join in ten seconds. Offices, for a quick break or a team social that doesn't need booking anything. Classrooms, where a teacher hosts on the projector and students join from their own phones. And family gatherings and holidays, where the age range is wide and "scan this and tilt your phone" is the only instruction anyone needs.

A concrete example: Steer It

Steer It is one game in this category — a browser racing party game where each phone becomes a tilt steering wheel. The host opens it on any screen, players scan a QR code, and up to eight cars drift around one shared screen.
